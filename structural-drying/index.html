Of all the stages in restoring water damage, drying the structure counts most, and it is the stage most often botched by people without training. After extraction removes the standing water, significant moisture remains trapped in structural materials, including drywall, wood framing, subflooring, concrete, insulation and other building components. A structure left wet ends up with odors, wood rot, mold and failing materials, so the moisture has to be removed in a scientific way using dehumidification and controlled evaporation.

Psychrometry is the science of how air temperature, humidity and moisture interact, and the IICRC-certified technicians who dry structures for Provo Restoration know it well. Using this knowledge, they calculate the specific equipment placement, temperature settings and airflow patterns needed to create the right evaporation conditions for each drying situation. Plaster, concrete, hardwood and drywall each dry at their own rate, so each needs its own approach to get down to an acceptable moisture level with no added damage.

Daily monitoring is built into how we dry a structure, and it relies on calibrated meters, thermal cameras and thermo-hygrometers. Readings are recorded from start to finish in a detailed drying log, which shows moisture dropping step by step in every affected material. Equipment is adjusted daily based on these readings to optimize drying efficiency. A surface that feels dry to your hand proves nothing, so the job is finished only once all affected materials test within the acceptable standards set by IICRC S500.

Why Hire a Professional for Structural Drying?

Drying a structure properly takes science, and guesswork has no place in it. Secondary mold damage most often traces back to someone who put down fans and hoped things would dry. To do it professionally, you have to know how each material takes on and gives up moisture, work out the grain depression, meaning the moisture removal capacity, for the job, and check progress every day. Get it wrong in one direction and mold feeds on leftover moisture, get it wrong in the other and wood or similar materials are damaged by over-drying.

Professional drying equipment is not a bigger version of what stores sell, it is a different kind of machine. Commercial LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers remove 10 to 20 times more moisture from the air than residential dehumidifiers, and high-velocity air movers create the precise airflow patterns needed to accelerate evaporation from structural surfaces. Machines like these cost a great deal and take training to use well, which explains why professional results are so much better than a do-it-yourself effort.

Maintenance Tips for Structural Drying

A little prevention goes a long way in avoiding expensive restoration emergencies. Follow these tips from our IICRC-certified technicians:

  • If a new damp spot or smell shows up while drying is underway, let the technician know at once
  • Make sure the technician can get in each day for monitoring visits
  • Shut the doors and windows of any room under drying, which keeps dehumidification working well
  • Leave the drying equipment on even if the noise bothers you, since each hour counts
  • The dehumidifiers and air movers should run around the clock until our technician takes them out
  • Address any water intrusion immediately, because the faster drying begins, the better the outcome

How to Tell You Need Structural Drying

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Ceilings, floors or walls that still read high on a moisture meter following a leak
  • Signs of deterioration where an old leak was left to dry on its own
  • No visible water left, yet walls or floors still feel cold and damp
  • Hardwood floors that are cupping, crowning or showing gaps between boards
  • Condensation forming on windows in the area that was wet
  • Warped casing or baseboard where water reached the bottom of the wall
  • Blistered paint and lifting wallpaper on a wall that got wet
